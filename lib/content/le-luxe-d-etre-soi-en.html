Consider the migratory bird held in a cage. When autumn comes, a fever lifts it. It stops sleeping, rises in the night, throws itself toward the patch of sky it ought to be cleaving, falls back and begins again. There is nothing disordered in this agitation, for nature has engraved the route and the hour into the creature, granting it the sovereign gift of knowing where to go at the very moment it commands the going. Its expanse could be taken from it without the order to depart ever being taken with it, so that it keeps the whole map and not one inch of sky.

Man carries an order of the same blood, more muffled, and has forgotten that it was ever handed to him. It enjoins him to catch up with himself, to become one day that part of himself he holds to be the truest, and it rises from within with the authority of what admits no discussion. Yet many pass through lives so well built that answering it becomes almost forbidden.

We would like to believe that at the bottom of a thwarted life there sleeps a true self, whole, captive, needing only to be released. It is not so simple, for there is no intact treasure crouching beneath the habits and waiting for its hour. What we call being oneself is not something one owns and might have mislaid. It is a way of living, and it has to be paid for.

There lies the real subject, the one we do not dare look at. Not what it costs to leave, but what it costs to stay. We have learned to dread the first price, which is brutal and visible, and never to reckon the second, which is slow and mute.

For our lives have been built on a tacit bargain. We give up time, momentum, whatever in us cannot be priced, and receive in return the security of a certain comfort, a loan we are able to service, a status that reassures, a place that holds. This bargain is nothing like a choice made alone, since an entire era offered it to everyone as the very shape of success, and we subscribed to it without so much as remembering that we had signed. It long seemed a good one, because it bought a kind of peace. And that peace has an underside we discover too late, for what makes us secure is often what holds us back, and what holds us back ends by carrying us away from ourselves.

This is the trap of the reassuring. The cage never presents itself as a cage. It wears the face of prudence, of responsibility, of the common sense that counsels against risking everything. We want another life, another bearing, and we answer ourselves at once that we cannot, that there is the loan, the position secured, everything we spent years building and everything we have become in the eyes of others. We raised our own walls, knotted our own chains, and we give the name of wisdom to the fear that makes us keep them. The jailer, in the end, wears the prisoner’s face.

The most troubling thing is not that we stay out of fear, but that we refuse to call it fear. The mind is skilled at dressing up what it dares not admit to itself, and it works tirelessly at turning dread into reason. A man who knows, deep down, that his work produces nothing of worth persuades himself that he is carrying out a mission there, serving a great cause, weighing on the course of things from the inside. We tell ourselves we are doing good where we are merely staying, and the justification burns all the brighter because part of us knows it to be false. We are not only the keepers of our cage, we are its counsel, and we plead its case with the ardour of someone defending an indefensible position.

And we reckon badly. Leaving presents itself as a single outlay, quantified, the security let go, the eyes of others, the void ahead. Staying never takes the appearance of an invoice, and that is its trap. The outlay is levied in silence, one year after another, on the only thing that cannot be repaid, which is life itself. The cage is paid for against the years one has left, in small sums we never feel go by, and the bill falls due only at the end, when nothing remains with which to settle it.

The bird makes no error in its accounts. It knows that staying would cost it more than the great wind of departure, and that is why it throws itself against the bars. We have unlearned that reckoning. We take stillness for wisdom, and we call reason the price we have stopped seeing.

There is, besides, an interest in our continuing to reckon badly. So long as each of us lives the cage as a private affair, a weakness of character, a courage that happened to be lacking, no one asks who built the cages or why they resemble one another so closely. The weariness of so many lives lined up side by side is not a sum of individual failures. It is the trace of an order that promised everyone the fulfilment of the self and reserved to a few the means of reaching it. To call this a want of courage is already to do that order a service, since pain believed to be private threatens nothing of what produces it.

There remains the only question worth asking, and it is not settled in a word. This life so prudent, so well kept, the one we dare not leave because it protects us, does it protect us still, or have we merely stopped counting what it takes. It happens that one realises too late that security and life had been confused, that the cage was kept in the belief that the sky was being kept. A bird is given back to itself by being given back the sky. It must still stop believing that the cage, deep down, protects it.
